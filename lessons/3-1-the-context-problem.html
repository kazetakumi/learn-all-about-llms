The context problem

Why one vector cannot hold a sentence, and the idea that replaced it.

Read this sentence once.

The teacher who was explaining a hard idea to the students smiled.

Who smiled? You know at once: the teacher. But look at the distances. The word students sits right next to smiled. The word teacher sits nine words back. Proximity points to the wrong answer, and you still got it right.

Before you read onHow did you get it right? What did your eyes or your memory actually do when you reached smiled?

Show what I think happened

You did not replay the whole sentence word by word. You reached smiled, asked "who?", and your attention went straight back to teacher. The question picked its own answer from the past. Hold on to that. The whole lesson is the attempt to give a machine that one ability.

One thing you need first

A network cannot read words. It reads numbers. So each token becomes a vector: a list of numbers, perhaps 768 of them. Think of a vector as an arrow in a space with many directions.

The useful fact is this: training puts words with similar meaning on arrows that point in similar directions. Apple, mango and orange point into one region. Cat, dog and cow point into another. That is all we need today. In the next crux we build the tool that measures "points in a similar direction".

If "vector" or "direction" feels shaky, say so. We open a short branch on vectors and the dot product, then come back here.

The job

The story of attention starts in 2014, with machine translation. The task: read an English sentence, write the French one. I use translation because the problem is easy to see there. But keep the language model in mind. A language model that writes the next word has exactly the same need. Each word it writes depends on words somewhere in the past, and it must find them.

So here is the design question. When the network writes output word number \(i\), where does it get the information about the input?

First attempt: a window

The simplest answer: look only at the last few words. A bigram model looks at one word back. An n-gram model looks at \(n-1\) words back.

Your turnUse the teacher sentence. How wide must the window be for the model to connect smiled with teacher? Then ask the harder question: what width is enough for every sentence?

Show the answer

Ten words for this sentence. And no width is enough for every sentence, because I can always add one more clause between teacher and smiled. A wider window also costs more: the count tables of an n-gram model grow with every extra word of width. A fixed window fails because the distance to the needed word has no limit.

Second attempt: a running summary

The next idea is clever. Read the sentence one word at a time. Keep one vector, the hidden state \(h\). At each word, mix the new word into \(h\):

\(h_t = f(x_t,\; h_{t-1})\)

This is a recurrent neural network, an RNN. Its memory is not a window. In principle, \(h\) can carry information from the first word to the last.

For translation, two RNNs work together. The encoder reads the English sentence and ends with a final state. That final state is the context vector \(c\). The decoder receives \(c\) and writes the French sentence, one word at a time. Sutskever and colleagues, and Cho and colleagues, built this design in 2014, and it worked surprisingly well.

The encoder–decoder before attention. The decoder sees only \(c\).

Predict before you lookThe context vector has a fixed size, say 1000 numbers, for every sentence. Sketch a curve: translation quality on the vertical axis, sentence length on the horizontal axis. What shape do you expect?

Show what happened

Quality rises for short sentences, then falls hard. An earlier study by Cho and colleagues had already reported it: the basic encoder–decoder gets rapidly worse as the input grows. Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio plotted it clearly. Their encoder–decoder peaks at about 20 words and then drops. At 50 to 60 words, the model trained on sentences up to 30 words scores about a tenth of its peak.

Two causes work together. First, capacity: a long sentence must fit into the same 1000 numbers as a short one. Second, recency: \(h\) is overwritten at every step, so early words fade most. Teacher came early. By the time the decoder needs it, little of it is left.

Bahdanau and colleagues named the cause in the first lines of their paper. They wrote that the fixed-length vector is "a bottleneck". I want you to see that this is a property of the design, not of the RNN. Any design that squeezes a sentence of any length into one vector of fixed size has this bottleneck. You will prove that yourself at the end of this lesson, with no RNN at all.

The fix: stop squeezing

Go back to what you did with the teacher sentence. You did not carry a summary to smiled. You kept the sentence available, and the question "who?" chose where to look.

Now turn that into a design, in three steps.

Step 1: keep every state. The encoder still reads the sentence. But we keep all the states \(h_1, \dots, h_T\), not only the last one. Nothing is squeezed. A long sentence simply gives more vectors.

Step 2: score each state for the current need. When the decoder is about to write word \(i\), its own state \(s_{i-1}\) says what it is doing now. A small network looks at \(s_{i-1}\) and one encoder state \(h_j\), and gives back one number: how relevant is word \(j\) right now? Call it \(e_{ij}\). How this small network works is the next crux. Today, treat it as a judge that outputs one score.

Step 3: mix by relevance. Turn the scores into weights that are positive and sum to 1. The tool for that is the softmax. Then build a fresh context vector as a weighted sum:

\(\alpha_{ij} = \dfrac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_k \exp(e_{ik})} \qquad c_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij}\, h_j\)

Look at the small letter \(i\) on \(c_i\). This is the whole idea. Before, one context vector \(c\) served every output word. Now each output word gets its own context vector \(c_i\), mixed for its own need. When the decoder writes chat, most of the weight goes to cat. When it writes the next word, the weights move.

Do not compress the past into one vector. Keep all of it, and for each step build a weighted look-back, with weights set by what that step needs.

Bahdanau and colleagues called this letting the decoder "pay attention" to parts of the source. The name stuck.

Why weights, and not a choice?

Here is a fair objection. If the decoder needs cat, why mix at all? Pick the single most relevant word and pass its vector on.

Your turnFind two reasons why a soft mixture is better than a hard pick. One reason is about language. The other is about how a network learns.

Show the two reasons

Language. Bahdanau's own example: English the man becomes French l'homme. To write l', the decoder must look at the and also at man. The noun decides the article: le, la, les or l'. A hard pick sees only one word. A mixture sees both.

Learning. A network learns by small nudges: change a weight a little, see if the loss goes down a little. A hard pick has no "a little". It flips from one word to another. So no signal flows back to tell the judge how to score better. Soft weights change smoothly, so the error signal flows back through them. Then the judge and the translator learn together. The paper says this directly: the soft alignment lets the gradient flow back through it.

"Gradient" and "nudges" are the subject of Part 0. If that step felt like a leap, it is a good branch to open later.

What the network learned

Nobody told the network which English word matches which French word. It only had to translate well. Yet the weights \(\alpha_{ij}\) show a matching. Bahdanau and colleagues drew them as a grid: French words down the side, English words across the top, bright cells for large weights.

PredictEnglish: the European Economic Area. French: la zone économique européenne. The French order is reversed. When the decoder writes zone, which English word gets the largest weight? And when it writes européenne?

Show the alignment

Zone looks at Area, three words ahead, and jumps over European and Economic. Then économique looks back one word to Economic, and européenne looks back one more to European. The bright cells run against the diagonal for this phrase. A window could not do this. A running summary would have to hold all three words at once. The look-back just goes where it is needed.

And the length curve? Their attention model trained on sentences up to 50 words, RNNsearch-50, shows no drop even at 50 words and more. On the full test set, its score is 26.75, against 17.82 for the encoder–decoder trained on the same sentences.

The price

Nothing is free. Ask yourself what we gave up.

Show the cost

Memory and work now grow with length. The summary design kept one vector per sentence. The look-back keeps one vector per word, and it scores every one of them for every output word. Twice the sentence means twice the vectors to keep, and twice the scores for each word written.

Remember this cost. In Part 1.5 it comes back twice. Once as the KV cache: the stored past becomes the limit on how fast a model can write. And once as the n² bill: when every word looks at every other word, the work grows with the square of the length. Most of the attention family is a list of ways to pay less of this price.

Your turn: break the bottleneck yourself

Here is the claim from before: the bottleneck is a property of any fixed-size summary, not of the RNN. Let's test it with no RNN at all.

The test: make a "sentence" of \(n\) random word vectors. Store it in two ways.

(a) One summary vector: add all \(n\) vectors into one vector of the same size. This is the most generous summary possible, because nothing is forgotten on purpose.
(b) Keep every vector: keep all \(n\) vectors, like the look-back.

Then ask each one a question a decoder needs: "Was this word in the sentence?" Half the test words were in it, half were not. To score a test word against a vector, the code uses the @ operator. It measures how much two arrows point the same way. You build it properly in the next crux.

Predict firstWrite down the accuracy you expect for (a) and (b) at 5, 20 and 160 words. Each vector has 64 numbers.

The code is in lessons/code/3_1_bottleneck.py. Run it with python3 lessons/code/3_1_bottleneck.py. It needs only NumPy.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
d = 64                       # size of every vector (the "memory budget")

def word():                  # a random word vector of length 1
    v = rng.standard_normal(d)
    return v / np.linalg.norm(v)

def trial(n):
    sentence = [word() for _ in range(n)]
    H = np.stack(sentence)                       # look-back: keep all n vectors
    summary = H.sum(axis=0)                      # bottleneck: squeeze into one vector
    probes = sentence + [word() for _ in range(n)]   # half were in the sentence, half were not
    truth = [True] * n + [False] * n
    right_summary = sum((p @ summary > 0.5) == t for p, t in zip(probes, truth))
    right_lookback = sum(((H @ p).max() > 0.5) == t for p, t in zip(probes, truth))
    return right_summary / (2 * n), right_lookback / (2 * n)

print(" words | one summary vector | keep every vector")
for n in [2, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160]:
    s, l = np.mean([trial(n) for _ in range(50)], axis=0)
    print(f"{n:6d} | {s:18.0%} | {l:17.0%}")

Guessing gives 50%. So at 160 words, the summary is barely better than a coin. The look-back stays perfect. Same words, same budget per vector. The only difference: squeeze or keep.

Why does the summary fail? Each stored word adds a little noise to every test. With more words, the noise grows until it hides the signal. Your curve has the same shape as Bahdanau's encoder–decoder curve, and you made it without any RNN.

Ablation: just make the vector bigger?

A natural engineer's answer: use a bigger summary vector. Change d = 64 to 256, and then to 1024.

It only moves the cliff. Look at the 82% cells: 20 words with \(d = 64\), and 80 words with \(d = 256\). Four times the vector buys four times the length. Accuracy depends on the ratio of words to size, \(n/d\). For any fixed size, a long enough input breaks it.

Bahdanau's table says the same thing from the other side. Their attention model trained on 30-word sentences beats the encoder–decoder trained on 50-word sentences. A better design beat more data.

Trouble. The ratio \(n/d\) is near 1, worse than the 160-word, \(d = 256\) case. Specific details from the middle are the first to drown in the noise. A look-back over the stored words does not have this problem.

2Suppose the decoder can look back, but every weight is equal: \(\alpha_{ij} = 1/T\) for all words. What have we built?

Show

A plain average of the past, the same for every output word. That is one fixed summary again. The power of attention is not the look-back alone. It is the selective look-back: weights that change with the current need. So everything depends on the scores \(e_{ij}\). How to compute a good score is the next crux.

3A GPT has no encoder and translates nothing. It only writes the next word of one text. Where does today's idea fit?

Show

The text it has written so far is its "source sentence". To write the next word, it looks back over its own past words with relevance weights. A sentence that attends to itself is called self-attention. Same idea, one sequence instead of two.

What you own now

One sentence, which you could now rebuild from nothing: a fixed-size summary of a past of any length must fail as the past grows, so keep the whole past and, for each step, mix it with weights set by that step's need.

Everything else in this part grows from that sentence. How to score relevance. How to make the scores learnable. How to stop a word from seeing the future. How to look for several things at once.
